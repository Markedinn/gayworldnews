History

Burying the Emperor's Boyfriends

Uzbekistan remains one of the final two post-Soviet states actively criminalizing consensual same-sex acts between men. Under current statutes, the police execute targeted raids, online entrapment operations, and forced examinations that carry prison terms of up to three years. For gay men, the territory represents a high-caution environment without civil protections.

So, how did Uzbekistan become so dangerous?

16th Century: The Emperor's Obsession

In the year 1499, the teenage royal conqueror Zahir al-Din Muhammad Babur took control of the Fergana Valley before building the global Mughal Empire. Far from hiding his desires, the young king fell hopelessly in love with a local teenage street vendor inside the busy military camp bazaar. Records show Babur was completely smitten and desperately obsessed, abandoning his royal duties to pursue the youth.

Source: Wikimedia Commons and the Baburnama Manuscript Archives

The imperial court ledger, preserved across centuries, notes that the object of the king's affection was Baburi Andijani. The teenage ruler experienced such a profound emotional breakdown over the youth that he began roaming the hills barefoot and bareheaded like a madman. Babur documented this absolute vulnerability and devotion directly in his personal diary, known globally as the Baburnama Manuscript Files.

"During this time, there was a [youth] from the camp market named Baburi. Even his name was amazingly appropriate. I developed a strange inclination for him, rather I made myself miserable over him."
The Baburnama Chronicle

Instead of facing religious execution or political exile, Babur retained his respect and sovereign power and eventually appointed his loved one to senior administrative positions within the royal retinue. The royal family and court officials fully accepted this deep bond, viewing it as a natural aristocratic privilege rather than an ideological crime. The couple remained together throughout the early military campaigns that reshaped Central Asia.

19th Century: The Celebrated Dancers of Samarkand

During the late nineteenth century, the historic silk road hubs of Bukhara and Samarkand openly maintained a highly organized network of celebrated male entertainers known as bachas. These young men were trained extensively in dance, music, and the arts of companionship for the wealthy male elite of the region. Local governors and wealthy merchants competed fiercely to secure the exclusive attention of the most prominent performers.

In the year 1873, the American diplomat, Eugene Schuyler, explored the newly conquered Russian territory of Turkistan to document local customs. He discovered that these male dancers were not outcasts but held an elevated social status where entire bazaars rose to salute them with hands over their hearts. Schuyler recorded these explicit details in his confidential diplomatic records, published later as the official report Turkistan Notes of a Journey.

The local community treated the top male performers with the exact same prestige given to legendary opera stars in Western Europe. When a performer walked through the market, shopkeepers considered it an immense honor if the youth stopped to rest in their establishment. Many of these young men eventually transitioned into highly respected, wealthy adult citizens who managed local trade guilds.
